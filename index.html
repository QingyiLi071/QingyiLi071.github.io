About Me

I am a second-year PhD student in Developmental Science at Virginia Tech.

I study how the brain is organized in the individual rather than in the group average, where that individual organization comes from, and what it predicts. My primary design is the parent–child dyad: measuring the same individualized features in parents and children lets me ask what is shared with the parent, through inheritance and family environment together, and what is specific to the child. My work runs along three lines:

  1. Individualized brain organization. I use precision functional mapping and morphometric similarity networks to decompose each child’s network topography into parent-shared and child-specific parts, and to measure parent–child similarity in cortical structure.
  2. Brain and affect dynamics. I use hidden Markov models and intensive longitudinal data to track how brain states and daily affect move over time, as the temporal counterpart of spatial organization.
  3. Individual-level risk. I test whether individualized features predict later internalizing symptoms and suicidal ideation, so far with daily affect and next with brain features, using cross-validated and externally validated models.

Ultimately, I hope this work will help advance precision psychiatry by grounding risk assessment and intervention in each person’s own brain organization and daily experience.

Fun fact: I have been asking these questions for as long as I can remember, first through the I Ching, BaZi, and Zi Wei Dou Shu, systems that also try to read a person against time and circumstance. Around 24, with a freshly matured prefrontal cortex, I switched my allegiance to science. I have not forgotten my old flame: in my spare time I still study these traditions and keep refining my technique.
